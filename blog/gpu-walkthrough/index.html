Train your own neural surrogate for N-body dynamics in an afternoon

2026-11-02 · Harsh Kumar · hands-on companion to the N-body surrogates work

You don't need a datacenter to make a neural network learn gravity. You need a simulation, a sliding window, and a model small enough to train before your coffee cools. This post walks through exactly that: a per-body MLP surrogate you can build this afternoon — and, more importantly, how to evaluate it without fooling yourself, which is where most attempts quietly fail.

Everything here scales up: the same skeleton is what my thesis work expanded to LSTM and GNN surrogates trained at body counts up to 100. The full benchmark lives at n-body-surrogates; the code below is the minimal sketch you'd write for yourself.

Step 0: what you need

  • Python + PyTorch, any recent version
  • A simulator (30 lines: leapfrog integration of N bodies, see any celestial mechanics primer — or use numpy alone)
  • A GPU? Not initially. A small per-body MLP trains in minutes on a laptop CPU. Rent one only when you scale to GNNs or thousands of trajectories — more in §6.

Step 1: generate trajectories

Train on simulated data. Draw N bodies with masses from a chosen initial mass function, random positions and velocities, integrate with leapfrog at fixed Δt, and store per-timestep states (x, y, z, vx, vy, vz). Freeze the dataset before you train and never regenerate it against a failing model — a regenerated dataset is a test set you'll contaminate without noticing.

Standardize every feature (positions and velocities separately, per axis). Surrogate training is miserable with unstandardized physics: positions span a parsec, velocities a few km/s, and MSE doesn't care equally about both.

Step 2: sliding windows, not whole trajectories

# W=5 sliding window: each sample is a (W, n_features) history
def make_windows(traj, W=5):
    xs, ys = [], []
    for t in range(W, len(traj) - 1):
        xs.append(traj[t-W:t])   # history
        ys.append(traj[t+1])     # next state
    return np.array(xs), np.array(ys)

This window shape is load-bearing. It matches deployment: at predict time the model always sees the last W states, whatever their source. Keep it identical between training and rollout, or your eval silently measures a different model than the one you trained.

Step 3: a per-body MLP

The trick that made the thesis work at variable N: model each body independently. Each body's network input is its own history plus (optionally) a small pooled summary of the others — masses, distances, relative vectors. One shared network, applied per body, handles N=10 or N=100 with the same parameters.

class PerBodyMLP(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, in_features=6*5, hidden=128):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(in_features, hidden), nn.GELU(),
            nn.Linear(hidden, hidden), nn.GELU(),
            nn.Linear(hidden, 6))          # predicts (x,y,z,vx,vy,vz) delta

    def forward(self, window):             # window: (B, W, 6)
        B, W, F = window.shape
        return self.net(window.flatten(start_dim=1))

Predict the delta to the last known state, not the absolute next state — residues are small, well-behaved, and the model starts near-correct instead of learning "space is big."

Step 4: train

model = PerBodyMLP().to DEVICE
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
loss_fn = nn.MSELoss()
for epoch in range(...):
    for xb, yb in loader:      # windows from MANY different trajectories
        xb, yb = xb.to(DEVICE), yb.to(DEVICE)
        pred = xb[:, -1, :6] + model(xb)     # delta on last state
        loss = loss_fn(pred, yb)
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

Mine your windows from many unrelated trajectories, not one long clip — window diversity is what makes the model generalize across systems. A few thousand windows is enough; training is minutes on CPU, seconds on any GPU.

Step 5: the evaluation that matters

This is the step people skip, and it's the one that separates a demo from a result (details in my rollout post):

  • Feed predictions back. Predict state t+1 from your window, append your prediction, slide forward. Teacher forcing (appending ground truth) will flatter the model by ×5–6 and tell you nothing about deployment.
  • Hold out real systems. If your training data is synthetic disc configurations, test on initial conditions the model never saw a family of — in the thesis work, real Solar-System ephemerides.
  • Report divergence, not just mean error. A rollout that explodes is a result, not a to-be-suppressed footnote.

§6: when does renting a GPU actually pay off?

Be honest with yourself about the scaling: an MLP on a few thousand windows is a CPU job. GPU rental earns its cost when you (a) move to GNN surrogates — my GNN was dramatically better in-distribution at high body counts — (b) widen your dataset to millions of windows, or (c) run sweep after sweep during experiment search. Then an on-demand GPU by the hour beats owning anything, since a couple of dollars of compute covers a full experiment day at the small end.

Same honesty applies in reverse: before renting anything, spend the laptop minutes and confirm your pipeline is right. The GPU multiplies velocity, not correctness.

That's the whole thing: simulate, window, per-body model, honest rollout. If you build something with it, tell me — and if your rollout finds a failure mode my benchmark doesn't show, that's exactly the data I want next.
