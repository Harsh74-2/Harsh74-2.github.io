Validating a dynamics surrogate out of distribution (without fooling yourself)

2026-11-02 · Harsh Kumar · distilled from the N-body surrogates audit

My surrogates scored test MSE of 1e-8 to 2e-7 in-distribution. Pointed at the real Solar System, full-system rollouts diverged for every model I trained, at every body count. Nothing about the in-distribution score predicted that.

This post is the checklist that gap forced me to build. It's specific to dynamical-system surrogates (physics, time series, control), but most of it transfers to any model that will see data unlike its training set. Test data comes from seven Solar-System presets built from NASA JPL Horizons ephemerides; training was a synthetic frozen-disc dataset. Every number below is from the public benchmark pages.

1. Evaluate in deployment mode, not training mode

Training feeds ground-truth history. Deployment feeds the model its own predictions. For my checkpoints the difference was a measured 4.7–6.3× error penalty, and it's a lower bound because diverged rollouts were excluded from the mean. If your eval loop imports anything from the training loop, audit it - mine had a recurrence hidden inside model.step() that silently mismatched the sliding-window protocol (W=5) the models were actually trained for.

2. Use real initial conditions, not resampled training data

Held-out test splits of a synthetic dataset measure interpolation, not generalization. Pull real initial states from an independent source - JPL Horizons gives ephemerides for free. The distribution shift this exposes is exactly the one production will see. In my case, OOD single-step error ranged from ~0.3% (best model, solar-system-extended preset) to 25–30% (inner-planets and sun-earth-only presets) - a spread invisible from in-distribution metrics, which all sat near 1e-8.

3. Normalize error by the system's own scale

Raw MSE across systems of different sizes is meaningless: an Earth-and-Sun system has a smaller characteristic length than the full Solar System, so the same absolute error is a different physical failure. I report every error as a percentage of the preset's scale length L. Deciding which presets are comparable becomes honest instead of decorative.

4. Define divergence and count it as data

“Diverged” must be a defined event - mine is error exceeding 100% of L during rollout - and it belongs in the result table, not a footnote. The full Solar-System preset diverged for every model at every N. Suppressing that row would have turned my summary from “a 5–6× penalty with complete failure on the hardest preset” into a much happier-sounding number that describes nothing a user would experience.

5. Beware survivor bias in the mean

Most presets kept only two surviving presets (inner planets, sun-earth-only). Averaged over survivors only, the OOD rollout mean looks like a tidy 63–73% plateau. But the N=100 LSTM averaged 97.9% over a single surviving preset - its “mean” is one near-death rollout. Always report, per cell, how many presets survived before you read the average.

6. Check scaling claims at both ends

The model that generalizes best might be the one you can't afford. My GNN had the best in-distribution long-horizon drift at N=100 (8.91e-04) but per-step latency grew from ~2 ms at N=10 to 33–68 ms at N=100, against the MLP's ~0.25 ms flat. If your deployment has a wall clock, latency-at-scale is part of the evaluation, not an implementation footnote.

The one-line summary

In-distribution scores measure the model. Out-of-distribution rollout measures the system - model plus feedback loop plus data distribution. Only the second number is the product.
