×5 in the dark: what autoregressive rollout actually costs

2026-11-02 · Harsh Kumar · part of the N-body surrogates work

My bachelor thesis trained neural surrogates — MLP, LSTM, and a graph neural network — to predict gravitational N-body dynamics. The models are good. Test MSE in-distribution sits at 1e-8 to 2e-7 for the MLP and LSTM. If you read only those numbers, you'd conclude the problem is solved.

It isn't, and the gap between the two headlines is one number: autoregressive feedback costs roughly 5–6× in accuracy — measured, and almost certainly a lower bound.

What the models are asked to do

Predictions are made one step at a time: the model sees a window of recent states and predicts the next one, which is fed back into the next window. That's how any real deployment works — the ground truth of the future isn't available while predicting it. The alternative, rebuilding each window from the reference trajectory, is teacher forcing: it flatters the model, because every step starts from a clean state.

I measured both, on the same trained checkpoints, across body counts N = 10, 25, 50, 100. The models were trained on a frozen disc initial mass function dataset; the evaluation set is real: seven Solar-System presets built from NASA JPL Horizons ephemerides, with a leapfrog integrator as reference.

Without feedback (reference-rebuilt windows), checkpoints average about 11–14% error. With autoregressive feedback, errors compound by a factor of ~4.7–6.3×.

That factor is a lower bound. Rollouts that diverged are excluded from the mean — the full Solar-System preset diverged for every model at every body count. Including them, the picture is worse.

No winner, and that was the result

Out-of-distribution rollout, averaged over presets with survivors, errors are reported as a percentage of each preset's characteristic scale length L:

ModelN=10N=25N=50N=100
MLP66.6%68.9%64.6%65.6%
LSTM64.7%70.8%64.0%diverged
GNN69.3%66.7%73.0%72.2%

Nearly every cell lands in a 63–73% band. Architecture doesn't matter. Body count doesn't matter. The task under feedback is essentially fail-or-plateau: either the rollout holds together for a while, or it comes apart entirely — and while it holds, error accumulates toward roughly two-thirds of the system scale regardless of how carefully the model was built. The N=100 LSTM is the cautionary tale: it diverged outright, and even the “stable” variant hit 97.9% error on the single preset that survived.

The GNN does win in-distribution long-horizon drift at high N (8.91e-04 vs much worse for the MLP/LSTM at K=50 rollout steps) — but pays for it in latency, scaling from ~2 ms at N=10 to 33–68 ms per step at N=100, against the MLP's ~0.25 ms. And none of that in-distribution advantage survived contact with the real Solar System under feedback.

The gotcha that quietly ruins your evaluation

One implementation detail cost me a full re-run. My GNN wraps recurrence inside a model.step() method — stateful, convenient, and wrong for out-of-distribution evaluation: it carries internal state across calls in a way that doesn't match the training-time distribution. The correct protocol is a stateless sliding window (W=5 in my setup): always feed the model the last 5 states, take the prediction, append it, slide forward. If your rollout numbers look suspiciously good, check whether the model is leaking internal state that training didn't teach it.

What I'd tell anyone building this

  • Report the compounding factor, not just endpoint error. “11% error” and “65% error” are the same model, with and without feedback. A paper that reports only one is describing deployment conditions it isn't honest about.
  • Report diverged cells. Excluding the full Solar-System row because “everything diverged” turns a 6× penalty into an optimistic summary. Divergence is the result.
  • Teacher forcing is a different experiment. Fine for diagnosing the model, misleading for estimating deployment accuracy.
  • Architectural complexity buys nothing under feedback. Three very different inductive biases converged to the same plateau. If feedback compounding is the bottleneck, the next dollar goes into the training objective (e.g. rollout/energy-aware losses) or error correction — not a bigger network.
